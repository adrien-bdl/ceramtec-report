\section*{Executive Summary}

CeramTec, a supplier of ceramic hip-bearing components to orthopaedic implant manufacturers, initially framed this project as \textbf{dynamic pricing}. After analysing the commercial context, we conclude that true dynamic pricing---continuous price adjustment in response to demand, inventory, or time-to-event, as in airlines or concert ticketing---is \textbf{not feasible nor desirable} for CeramTec. The company negotiates individually with a \textbf{small set of strategic OEM customers} (fourteen key-account-priority OEMs in the large and medium tiers, plus six high-potential accounts per CeramTec's internal register); prices cannot change hourly or daily. The relevant problem is \textbf{price stratification}: establishing evidence-backed \textbf{baseline price ranges} by market and customer to support future negotiations.

Because CeramTec had no prior external-data capability and internal sales data were only accessible from \textbf{7 July 2026} (NDA delays), a substantial share of the project focused on building a reproducible external evidence foundation across four priority markets (US, China, France, Italy). We designed and implemented a complete \textbf{four-phase pricing framework}---from evidence collection through feature engineering, segmentation, and predictive negotiation baselines.

This report therefore delivers three things: (i)~a critical reframing of the client's problem; (ii)~a step-by-step four-phase pricing framework implemented end-to-end; and (iii)~a maintainable evidence and modelling pipeline (external register, feature matrix, OEM segments, and price baselines). The \textbf{main binding constraint} proved to be external data retrieval in regulated healthcare markets, not the soundness of the pricing methodology itself.

\section{Introduction}

CeramTec is a global manufacturer of advanced technical ceramics and a leading supplier of ceramic components for orthopaedic implants. This report assesses whether a data-driven pricing approach can improve CeramTec's commercial decisions for ceramic hip bearings across markets and customers. We evaluate the client's initial ``dynamic pricing'' framing, argue for \textbf{price stratification} as the appropriate objective (Section~\ref{sec:reframing}), and present a four-phase framework with the external evidence base built in Phase~1.

A total hip implant is a prosthetic device that replaces a damaged hip joint to restore mobility and relieve pain. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:hip_implant}, the implant consists of several components, including the femoral stem, femoral head, acetabular shell, and acetabular liner. The bearing is the articulating interface between the femoral head and the acetabular liner, enabling smooth movement of the joint.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{introduction/hip_implant.png}
\caption{Components of a total hip implant.}
\label{fig:hip_implant}
\end{figure}

The choice of bearing material (such as ceramic-on-ceramic, ceramic-on-polyethylene, or metal-on-polyethylene) has a significant impact on wear performance, implant longevity, revision risk, and long-term patient outcomes. These characteristics also influence the economic value of the implant, making the bearing a key driver of both clinical outcomes and pricing decisions.

Although CeramTec currently prices its products primarily using a cost-based approach, it operates across markets with widely different reimbursement systems, procurement mechanisms, competitive environments, and customer profiles. These differences suggest that a uniform pricing strategy may not fully capture customers' willingness to pay or the value delivered by ceramic bearings. Furthermore, CeramTec serves multiple OEM customers with different market positions and product portfolios, creating additional opportunities for differentiated pricing.

The objective of this project is to assess the feasibility of a \textbf{data-driven pricing strategy} for CeramTec's ceramic hip bearings and to deliver a reproducible evidence base that the company can maintain after the consulting engagement. Section~\ref{sec:reframing} reframes the client's initial ``dynamic pricing'' expectation; Section~\ref{sec:methodology} introduces our four-phase framework; Phase sections report implementation progress, constraints, and mitigations.

\section{Project Scope and Methodology}

This section states the project objective, reframes the client's initial problem statement, introduces the four-phase methodology, and defines geographic and data scope.

\subsection{Problem reframing: price stratification versus dynamic pricing}
\label{sec:reframing}

The client initially described the project as \textbf{dynamic pricing}. In industries such as aviation, rail, and event ticketing, dynamic pricing means adjusting prices frequently---sometimes hourly---based on remaining capacity, time to departure or performance, and observed demand elasticity. Algorithms continuously re-optimise list prices to fill inventory at maximum willingness-to-pay.

CeramTec's commercial reality is fundamentally different:
\begin{itemize}
  \item CeramTec supplies \textbf{components} to a tiered set of OEM customers (fourteen key-account-priority OEMs in the large and medium tiers, plus six high potentials), with whom it maintains long-term, relationship-driven contracts.
  \item Prices are negotiated \textbf{individually} per account and product line; they are not published on a consumer-facing channel that could be repriced every minute.
  \item Frequent automated price changes would undermine trust with strategic buyers and are incompatible with medical-device contracting cycles.
\end{itemize}

We therefore reframe the problem as \textbf{price stratification} (or \textbf{negotiation baselines}): determining an initial \textbf{price range} or anchor for each relevant market--customer combination, grounded in cost pressure, payer ceilings, competitive intensity, clinical value, and account characteristics. CeramTec can then use these baselines as a starting point in negotiations rather than relying solely on production cost plus a uniform margin.

This reframing is critical for two reasons. First, it shows that we understood the client's true constraint rather than importing a fashionable ``dynamic pricing'' label from unrelated industries. Second, it sets realistic success criteria for the project: delivering a \textbf{framework and evidence base} for stratified pricing, not a real-time pricing bot.

\section{Phase 1: Data Collection and Exploration}
\label{sec:phase1}

Ceramtec currently collects data from two sources: from a Salesforce system for the demand and meeting with the clients, and from an SAP infrastructure for the effective commercial records (number of sales, sales prices, customer name, etc). However, Phase 1 starts from a clear initial situation: CeramTec had no centralized external-data system for pricing intelligence. Relevant signals existed across public institutions, registries, procurement portals, reimbursement schedules, macro databases, and company disclosures, but they were fragmented, inconsistent in format, and difficult to use in negotiation timelines.

Our solution was to build an end-to-end external evidence pipeline across all key pricing dimensions. Instead of collecting one-off files, we organized sources into a unified register and implemented reproducible retrieval and processing scripts so the dataset can be refreshed, extended, and maintained over time. This turns Phase 1 from a static deliverable into a durable capability that can keep tracking market evolution after the project period. Combined with the data already collected internally, we expect to encompass all the factors that can define the sale prices of the different products.

\subsection{Categorisation of the data}

The external evidence base is structured into 13 categories covering the full pricing logic: FX, material and energy cost pressure, reimbursement ceilings, public procurement prices, waiting times, surgery volumes, bearing mix, revision and clinical evidence, macro health spending, medtech benchmarks, OEM fundamentals, and competitor context. This structure ensures each pricing hypothesis can be linked to a specific and traceable evidence family rather than ad hoc assumptions.

At the register level, the current synchronized version tracks 51 rows of sources and datasets across these categories. Beyond the raw count, the workload was substantial because each source required separate handling rules (API pulls, structured CSV retrieval, PDF extraction, registry harmonization, or proxy construction where official data was incomplete). The result is a broad multi-market evidence map rather than a narrow single-dataset analysis.

Data acquisition was particularly difficult in healthcare due to uneven transparency and highly heterogeneous publication formats. Open machine-readable datasets are limited for several critical topics, many key values are embedded in long institutional PDFs, and time coverage can be irregular by country and year. In practice, this required country-specific retrieval strategies, explicit quality flags, and carefully documented partial/proxy coverage where full registry data was not available.

\subsection{Exploratory data analysis}

The exploratory analysis phase translated the retrieved evidence into first-order market signals. Initial plots focused on identifying drivers that plausibly explain pricing pressure and negotiation context: procedure volume trajectories, bearing-mix evolution, reimbursement ceilings versus observed procurement dynamics, and input-cost indicators (materials, energy, FX). This first signal layer is not intended as a final causal model but it establishes which variables are most promising for deeper pricing segmentation in later phases. Moreover, by exploring the data we managed to retrieved and by exchanging with Ceramtec's marketing and commercial teams about these results, we were able to really understand the market in which they operates and identify drivers that we weren't expecting.

Exploration also surfaced the \textbf{heterogeneity of healthcare systems} as a first-order modelling constraint. Reimbursement in France combines episode tariffs (GHS) with device-level LPPR caps; Italy's regional DRG and prosthetic lists fragment what appears as a single national market; the US splits Medicare DRG episode logic from commercial payer dynamics; China couples implant ceilings to VBP bidding rounds with differentiated CoC/CoP/MoP categories. Any price stratification model must treat \textbf{country} as a structural dimension, not a simple categorical label.

\subsection{Maintainability and durability of the solution}

Maintainability was a design requirement from the start. We implemented reusable scraping and processing scripts so data refreshes can be run as a standard workflow rather than rebuilt manually each cycle. Concretely, the project now supports one-command orchestration for retrieval and processing, plus a synchronized external register that documents source status, market scope, and update frequency. A merged country--year dataset (\texttt{external\_data\_dataset.csv}) combines market-panel indicators, collaborator procurement extracts (US SAM, France BOAMP), and USD-referenced FX and material-cost indices. This makes it feasible to ingest new releases regularly, preserve methodological consistency, and keep pricing evidence current after the project ends.

\subsection{Constraints encountered and solutions}

The main Phase~1 challenge was \textbf{external data retrieval}. CeramTec had no prior external-data capability; meanwhile healthcare markets offer few open, machine-readable datasets. Most relevant information is scattered across lengthy PDF reports (national registries, reimbursement schedules, agency publications) with non-standard layouts and uneven coverage by country and year. US and Italian registry extracts proved relatively rich; China often required proxy constructions; comparability across OEMs and markets cannot be assumed.

To scale collection, we developed \textbf{AI-assisted scraping and processing scripts} that retrieve reports from public portals, extract tables from PDFs (e.g.\ RIAP, AJRR, Open LPP), and harmonise outputs into tidy CSVs. This agentic approach materially improves throughput versus manual extraction, but remains imperfect: extracted values require \textbf{manual validation}, report formats change between years, and there is \textbf{no guarantee of parity} across countries. We document coverage explicitly in the External Data Register (Retrieved / Partial / Todo flags). Despite these limits, Phase~1 delivered the evidence backbone on which Phases~2--4 depend.


\section{Phase 2: Feature Engineering and Internal Data Integration}
\label{sec:phase2}

Phase~2 translates raw CeramTec internal records and the Phase~1 external panel into a \textbf{negotiation feature matrix} at the grain of one row per (country $\times$ customer/OEM). 

\subsection{Data sources and integration logic}

Three data layers are joined:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{SAP billing} --- transactional revenue, unit volumes, realised prices, product-group mix, and sales-lead ownership. Filtered to priority markets US, CN, FR, IT.
  \item \textbf{Salesforce exports} --- meeting counts, opportunity pipeline (won/lost/open), task activity, and win rate. SAP and Salesforce share \textbf{no common customer key}; accounts are matched via normalised name similarity. Unmatched SAP rows retain \texttt{sf\_is\_known=0}.
  \item \textbf{External country panel} --- latest-year THA volumes, ceramic-bearing share, reimbursement ceilings, material-cost indices, FX, and procurement intensity, joined on country code.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Feature families}

The matrix comprises 55 columns grouped into six families aligned with hip-implant negotiation logic:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{SAP commercial} --- total revenue, pieces sold, weighted average unit price, price volatility and monthly trend, revenue CAGR and last-year YoY, active months/years, COVID-era activity flag.
  \item \textbf{Product portfolio} --- number of product groups, dominant group, revenue shares across revision bearings, CLock, and Forte lines (reflecting CeramTec's hip-specific product architecture).
  \item \textbf{Contract continuity} --- inferred contract periods from billing gaps ($>$3 months); first/last period prices, renewal discount (\%), price slope across renewals, portfolio additions/drops, and months since last billing.
  \item \textbf{Salesforce engagement} --- meeting and opportunity counts, new-business meetings, win rate, days since last meeting.
  \item \textbf{External market context} --- country-level reimbursement, THA volume, ceramic share, material basket, procurement notices, China VBP-era flag.
  \item \textbf{Structural flags} --- country dummies (US/CN/FR/IT), \texttt{is\_covid\_era\_customer}, \texttt{is\_china\_vbp\_customer}, \texttt{is\_post\_covid\_customer}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Results}

The final matrix contains \textbf{84 country--customer rows} across priority markets: China (43), US (19), France (14), Italy (8). Cumulative SAP revenue in the extract is approximately EUR\,704M; median realised unit price is EUR\,190. Salesforce activity is matched for \textbf{67 of 84 accounts} (80\%).

\subsection{Constraints encountered and solutions}

Phase~2 was constrained primarily by \textbf{late access to internal data}. Historical sales and pricing existed at CeramTec throughout the project, but NDA clearance delayed handover until \textbf{7 July 2026}, compressing validation time within the academic timeline.

Additional integration barriers remain:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Salesforce} records demand and meetings but contains \textbf{no revenue data}.
  \item \textbf{SAP} holds transactional detail (pieces sold, revenue, customer, material, geography) but \textbf{cannot be linked to Salesforce} without a shared customer key; fuzzy name matching resolves most but not all accounts.

\section{Phase 3: Market and Customer Segmentation}

Phase~3 applies unsupervised clustering to the Phase~2 feature matrix to derive \textbf{pricing segments}---groups of OEM accounts with similar commercial scale, engagement intensity, and market exposure.

\subsection{Methodology}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Feature selection} --- 32 numeric columns after excluding identifiers, zero-variance fields, and country dummies (so geography does not dominate the distance metric; country is visualised separately).

\section{Phase 4: Price Baseline Model and Negotiation Support}
\label{sec:phase4}

Phase~4 translates the Phase~2 feature matrix and Phase~3 segments into \textbf{predictive negotiation baselines}: a point estimate of realised bearing unit price per country--OEM account, plus a symmetric uncertainty band derived from out-of-sample forecast errors. The objective is not automated repricing but an evidence-backed \textbf{anchor range} that commercial teams can use as a starting position in contract discussions.

\subsection{Modelling setup}

\textbf{Target.} Weighted average realised unit price (\EUR{} per bearing component), aggregated from SAP billing over the observation window.

\textbf{Predictors.} Forty numeric features from Phase~2, deliberately excluding price-derived columns (historical price volatility, contract-period price slopes, cluster PCA scores) to limit target leakage. Predictors capture commercial scale (revenue, volume, active years), product-portfolio breadth, contract continuity, Salesforce engagement, country dummies, and structural flags (COVID-era, China VBP).

\textbf{Models.} Four supervised learners spanning linear and non-linear families:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ridge} and \textbf{Lasso} regression (L2/L1 regularisation, standardised features),
  \item \textbf{Random Forest} (ensemble of shallow trees, robust to outliers),
  \item \textbf{XGBoost} (gradient-boosted trees, captures non-linear interactions).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Validation.} Five-fold cross-validation with hyperparameter grid search (optimising mean absolute error). Out-of-fold (OOF) predictions provide unbiased diagnostics; residual quantiles (10th--90th percentile) define negotiation bands around the point baseline.

\subsection{Negotiation playbook integration}

Phase~4 outputs connect directly to the three-tier playbook from Phase~3:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Strategic tier} (Cluster~2) --- use model baseline as floor; defend premium with volume and clinical-value evidence; band width reflects higher negotiation uncertainty on large accounts.
  \item \textbf{Core tier} (Cluster~0) --- apply segment-median baseline (EUR${\approx}$194); index adjustments to Phase~1 cost and reimbursement signals; flag accounts where actual price deviates ${>}$1 band width from prediction for review.
  \item \textbf{Development / exit tier} (Cluster~1, outliers) --- set aspirational anchor at upper band; require minimum volume commitment before matching core-tier pricing.
\end{enumerate}

Country-specific payer ceilings (Section~\ref{sec:phase1}) remain a \textbf{hard cap} on what OEMs can pass through; the model baseline should be interpreted as CeramTec's component-price anchor, not the hospital-facing implant price.

\subsection{Constraints encountered}

Phase~4 limitations reflect the small cross-sectional sample and data-integration gaps:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sample size} --- 84 observations limit model complexity and inflate variance on holdout folds; external financial features (SEC filings, registry microdata) available in Phase~1 were not yet joined at account level.
  \item \textbf{No temporal holdout} --- prices are aggregated over the full SAP window rather than forecast forward to a future contract year; a panel extension (account $\times$ year) would enable true out-of-time validation.
  \item \textbf{Scenario analysis} --- FX shocks, material-cost inflation, and reimbursement reforms (planned scenario views) require refreshing Phase~1 country indicators and re-running inference; not yet automated.
  \item \textbf{CRM gap} --- 17 accounts lack Salesforce features; predictions for these rows rely primarily on SAP commercial signals.
\end{itemize}

Despite these limits, Phase~4 closes the loop of the four-phase framework: external evidence (Phase~1) feeds negotiation features (Phase~2), which define segments (Phase~3) and now produce quantitative baseline ranges (Phase~4) that CeramTec can refine as internal data quality improves.


\section{Project Learnings}
\label{sec:learnings}

\subsection{Healthcare market and regulation}

We gained deep exposure to how \textbf{medical-device pricing is mediated by institutions}, not by spot markets. Payers rarely reimburse ``ceramic'' as a separate line item; instead, episode budgets and device lists constrain what hospitals can pay OEMs, who in turn pressure component suppliers. Cross-country comparison requires understanding \textbf{money-in} (reimbursement) and \textbf{money-out} (procurement/tender) simultaneously. China's VBP regime, Italy's regional fragmentation, and France's dual GHS/LPPR logic illustrate why a single global pricing formula fails. This institutional literacy is essential for credible price stratification with CeramTec's sales leadership.

\subsection{Technical: agentic AI for data engineering}

On the technical side, we learned to deploy \textbf{agentic AI at scale} for scraping and PDF extraction workflows---generating and iterating retrieval scripts, registry parsers, and validation notebooks under version control. The approach accelerates coverage of unstructured sources but demands disciplined human review and explicit \textbf{partial/todo flags} in the register. For CeramTec, the lasting value is a \textbf{maintainable pipeline culture}: new registry PDFs or reimbursement updates are ingested through scripts, not ad hoc spreadsheets.


\section{Conclusion}

The objective of this project was to assess the \textbf{feasibility of a data-driven pricing strategy} for CeramTec's ceramic hip bearings. We conclude that such a strategy is \textbf{feasible in principle} and that all four phases of the framework have been implemented: external evidence collection, feature engineering, OEM segmentation, and predictive negotiation baselines. On the 84-account internal sample, the best baseline model (XGBoost) achieves cross-validated MAE of EUR\,15.9 (${\approx}$8.7\% of median price)---sufficient as a negotiation anchor subject to commercial review and country-level payer constraints.

We reframed the client's initial ``dynamic pricing'' expectation into \textbf{price stratification}: baseline ranges for CeramTec's tiered OEM relationships across four regulated markets, not minute-by-minute repricing. Residual integration work---a shared SAP--Salesforce customer key and key-account register reconciliation---would improve feature completeness but does not block use of the current baseline outputs.

The \textbf{dominant challenge} was \textbf{external data retrieval}. Healthcare pricing signals are buried in institution-specific PDFs and national registries with heterogeneous formats; open APIs are scarce and cross-country parity is limited. AI-assisted scraping and processing materially improved scalability, yet manual validation and partial coverage remain unavoidable. This data environment---not model design---is the binding constraint on moving from framework to live pricing recommendations.

For CeramTec, we recommend: (i)~maintain and refresh the external register on a schedule; (ii)~establish a customer master key and complete the SAP/Salesforce join; (iii)~deploy the Phase~4 baseline bands in upcoming negotiations, using Phase~3 segments to tailor playbook language; and (iv)~use stratified negotiation baselines rather than pursuing literal dynamic pricing. Extending the model to an account--year panel and adding scenario views for cost and reimbursement shocks are the highest-value next steps.
