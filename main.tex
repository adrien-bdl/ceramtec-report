\section*{Executive Summary}

CeramTec can benefit from a \textbf{data-driven pricing strategy}, but not from real-time dynamic pricing. Its prices are negotiated with a concentrated group of original equipment manufacturer (OEM) customers through long-term commercial relationships, rather than adjusted continuously as in airlines or ticketing. We therefore recommend \textbf{evidence-based negotiation baselines} and \textbf{next-quarter price guidance}: use evidence on each customer's purchasing behaviour, relationship history, market environment, and product portfolio to prepare customer- and product-level anchors and identify the arguments that can best defend value.

A major source of business value is the creation of CeramTec's first structured \textbf{external market-data capability}. Before this engagement, the company had not systematically collected external evidence to complement its internal SAP and Salesforce records. We assembled fragmented public data across reimbursement and procurement, surgery volumes and waiting times, bearing preferences, clinical and revision outcomes, input-cost indicators, OEM performance, and competitive activity in the US, China, France, and Italy. This broader market view enables CeramTec to identify emerging trends, country-specific pricing pressure, and previously underexplored commercial opportunities that cannot be detected from internal transactions alone. The accompanying automated retrieval, cleaning, and aggregation pipelines turn this work into a repeatable capability rather than a one-off analysis.

The analysis confirms that customer size alone is not sufficient for pricing decisions. CeramTec's existing segmentation remains useful for allocating account-management resources, but customers with similar revenue can display materially different purchasing, engagement, and negotiation behaviours. The project therefore identifies four complementary pricing-behaviour segments and provides next-quarter price guidance at customer and product level. The principal commercial insight is that realised prices are strongly shaped by prior pricing, market position, purchasing volume, and relationship maturity. This supports a more consistent approach to renewal discussions, volume-for-discount trade-offs, price-erosion warnings, and the pricing of customers with limited history.

We recommend that CeramTec first validate the proposed segments and pricing drivers with Key Account Managers, then test the guidance for one or two negotiation cycles under \textbf{shadow conditions}, without making it binding on sales teams. Before operational deployment, production costs, contractual terms, foreign-exchange exposure, duties, and commercial judgement should be incorporated so that predicted market prices can be translated into margin-aware recommendations. The framework should remain a decision-support tool with human review, not an automated quoting engine.

Together, these deliverables form a reproducible four-phase framework covering market intelligence and data integration, feature engineering, customer segmentation, and predictive pricing support. The foundation can be refreshed and extended as new internal and external evidence becomes available.

\section{Introduction}

CeramTec is a global manufacturer of advanced technical ceramics and a leading supplier of ceramic components for orthopaedic implants. This report assesses whether a data-driven pricing approach can improve CeramTec's commercial decisions for ceramic hip bearings across markets and customers. We evaluate the client's initial ``dynamic pricing'' framing, argue for \textbf{evidence-based negotiation baselines} and \textbf{next-quarter price guidance} as the appropriate objective (Section~\ref{sec:reframing}), and present a four-phase framework spanning evidence collection, feature engineering, customer segmentation, and predictive negotiation support.

A total hip implant replaces a damaged hip joint to restore mobility and relieve pain. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:hip_implant}, the implant comprises a femoral stem, femoral head, acetabular shell, and acetabular liner. The bearing is the articulating interface between the femoral head and the acetabular liner.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{1_introduction/hip_implant.png}
\caption{Components of a total hip implant.}
\label{fig:hip_implant}
\end{figure}

Bearing material choice (ceramic-on-ceramic, ceramic-on-polyethylene, or metal-on-polyethylene) materially affects wear, fracture risk, revision outcomes, and therefore economic value. CeramTec currently prices primarily on a cost-plus basis, yet operates across markets with different reimbursement systems, procurement regimes, competitive intensity, and OEM profiles. These differences create headroom for differentiated negotiation baselines by market and customer.

\section{Business Context}
\label{sec:business-context}

\subsection{CeramTec in the value chain}

CeramTec's Medical Technology division recorded approximately EUR\,335M of the group's EUR\,685M revenue in 2025, with roughly 3{,}500 employees and 16 production sites. In hip arthroplasty, CeramTec operates as a \textbf{B2B component supplier}: it manufactures high-performance ceramic femoral heads and acetabular liners that OEMs integrate into complete implant systems. Hospitals negotiate with OEMs under country-specific reimbursement and procurement rules. CeramTec's pricing problem is therefore two-level---defending component value to OEM buyers whose own margins are capped by payer ceilings, tenders, and episode budgets.

Figure~\ref{fig:value-chain} summarises the commercial chain from component supply through to the patient. CeramTec ships ceramic heads and liners to OEMs, which design and brand the full implant system (examples include Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, DePuy, Smith+Nephew, Exactech, and MicroPort). Finished implants typically reach hospitals through regional, hospital-facing distributors; the hospital is the surgical end-use setting, and the patient is the ultimate clinical beneficiary. CeramTec does not sell bearings directly to hospitals or patients, so negotiation leverage depends on OEM willingness-to-pay under those downstream constraints.

Typical cost bands illustrate the value stack: a hip replacement procedure may cost EUR\,16k--47k; a complete implant roughly EUR\,2.6k--8.7k; a ceramic bearing about EUR\,289--419 in CeramTec SAP data (2020--2026); and a standard non-ceramic bearing roughly EUR\,73--270. Ceramic bearings therefore sit at the high-performance end of a cost-sensitive episode budget.

\subsection{Hip bearing products}

The hip portfolio centres on BIOLOX\textregistered{} forte (alumina) and BIOLOX\textregistered{} delta (zirconia-toughened alumina), supplied into CoP and CoC bearing couples. CoC minimises polyethylene wear but requires precise engineering; CoP remains the volume segment in most markets. Registry and VBP-proxy evidence collected in Phase~1 shows materially different ceramic-involved shares across US, Italy, France, and China---directly relevant to CeramTec's addressable premium volume.

\subsubsection{Current pricing approach}

CeramTec currently prices bearings primarily on a \textbf{cost-plus} basis: production cost plus a margin. Informal differentiation already appears in realised SAP prices through volume discounts and account history, but there is no systematic, evidence-backed baseline by market and negotiation behaviour. Given payer ceilings, procurement competition, and OEM heterogeneity, a single global cost-plus formula cannot capture willingness-to-pay differences across France's GHS/LPPR logic, Italy's regional fragmentation, US Medicare/commercial split, or China's Volume-Based Procurement (VBP) rounds.

\section{Project Scope and Methodology}

\subsection{Problem reframing: negotiation baselines versus dynamic pricing}
\label{sec:reframing}

Dynamic pricing in aviation or ticketing adjusts prices frequently in response to remaining capacity and time-to-event. CeramTec's reality differs: contracts are long-term and relationship-based with a concentrated OEM set; prices are negotiated individually rather than published on a continuously repriced channel; and frequent automated changes would undermine trust and conflict with medical-device contracting cycles.

We therefore reframe the objective as \textbf{evidence-based negotiation baselines} and \textbf{next-quarter price guidance}: customer- and product-level anchors informed by market context, purchasing behaviour, and relationship history. Commercial teams can use these as a starting position in negotiation---not as a small set of fixed group prices, and not as a real-time pricing bot.

\subsection{Scope}

\textbf{Objective.} Assess feasibility of a data-driven pricing strategy and deliver a maintainable framework for negotiation baselines.\\
\textbf{Markets.} USA, China, France, Italy.\\
\textbf{Accounts.} Full SAP hip-bearing customer universe in those markets (82 country--customer rows), with CeramTec's Key Accounts register as the strategic overlay.\\

\section{Phase 1: Data Collection and Exploration}
\label{sec:phase1}

CeramTec already collected internal commercial data in SAP and behavioural CRM data in Salesforce, but had no centralised external-data system for pricing intelligence. Phase~1 built that evidence backbone: a unified External Data Register, reproducible retrieval and processing scripts, and exploratory analysis across the four priority markets.

\subsection{Categorisation of the data}

External evidence is organised into 13 categories covering the full pricing logic: FX, material and energy cost pressure, reimbursement ceilings, public procurement prices, waiting times, surgery volumes, bearing mix, revision and clinical evidence, macro health spending, medtech benchmarks, OEM fundamentals, and competitor context. The synchronised register tracks 51 source rows (Appendix~\ref{app:external-data-register}).

Internal data complement this external layer. SAP provides the transactional commercial record---55{,}240 invoiced transactions across 170 customers in 25 countries and about EUR\,2.17B cumulative revenue in the broader extract---with hip-bearing analysis later restricted to Delta/Forte divisions in US/CN/FR/IT. Salesforce supplies the behavioural layer: roughly 1{,}500 records across six report exports, 223 accounts, and 380 meeting notes describing who is engaged, how often, and with what pipeline outcome.

\subsection{Exploratory data analysis}

EDA translated retrieved evidence into first-order market signals. Four findings structure later modelling.

\paragraph{Market heterogeneity is structural.}
Only some markets expose device-level prices. France and selected China VBP categories isolate device or implant ceilings more clearly than US episode DRGs or Italian regional lists. Any pricing-guidance model must treat country as a structural dimension, not a cosmetic label.

\paragraph{Bearing mix differs sharply by market.}
Figure~\ref{fig:bearing_mix} shows hip bearing-surface shares. Ceramic involvement is high and rising in China VBP demand data, while US AJRR remains more CoP/MoP oriented and European registries show intermediate mixes. These patterns affect CeramTec's addressable premium volume.

\paragraph{Demand is volume-capped.}
THA procedure volumes and waiting-time pressures indicate that demand is constrained by procedure throughput rather than by spot willingness-to-pay (Figure~\ref{fig:tha_volume}). This supports negotiation frameworks based on volume commitments and contract continuity rather than high-frequency price changes.

\paragraph{Internal prices already encode volume discounts.}
SAP ceramic-ball pricing shows strong dispersion: the first price decile is about twice the tenth, while the tenth decile buys roughly $52\times$ the volume of the first at about 50\% lower unit price. Salesforce notes reinforce four recurring negotiation dimensions: component type, order size, customer market (especially China competition), and local alternatives. Demand appears near-inelastic at the component level; volume discounting (order of $\sim$30\%) is the dominant commercial lever already in use.

\subsection{Maintainability and durability of the solution}

Maintainability was a design requirement. Retrieval and processing scripts support refreshable workflows; the external register documents source status and update frequency; and a merged country--year dataset (\texttt{external\_data\_dataset.csv}) combines market-panel indicators with FX and material indices. New registry releases can be ingested through scripts rather than ad hoc spreadsheets.

\subsection{Constraints encountered and solutions}

The binding Phase~1 constraint was \textbf{external data retrieval} in regulated healthcare markets: scarce open APIs, PDF-heavy registries, uneven country/year coverage, and China VBP proxies rather than a full clinical registry. AI-assisted scraping improved throughput but still requires human validation and explicit Retrieved / Partial / Todo flags. Despite these limits, Phase~1 delivered the evidence backbone on which Phases~2--4 depend.

\section{Phase 2: Feature Engineering and Internal Data Integration}
\label{sec:phase2}

Phase~2 converts the 3 sources of raw data into meaningful features that reflect the market dynamic.

\subsection{Data sources and integration logic}

SAP billing contributes revenue, quantity, realised unit price, product group, and sales ownership, filtered to the priority markets and hip divisions (Delta/Forte). Salesforce exports add meetings, opportunities, tasks, and win rates; because SAP and Salesforce share no common key, accounts are matched by normalised name similarity, and unmatched SAP rows retain \texttt{sf\_is\_known=0}. The external country panel joins THA volumes, ceramic share, reimbursement, FX, materials, and related indicators on billing country, using one-year lags where these fields enter prediction.

\subsection{Two modelling datasets}

Rather than forcing both analytical questions into one table, Phase~2 produces two datasets whose granularity matches their intended use (Table~\ref{tab:phase2_datasets}). Phase~3 uses a customer-level view to identify accounts with similar pricing and negotiation behaviours, allowing CeramTec to develop differentiated commercial playbooks. Phase~4 requires a more granular temporal panel: it learns from historical customer--product price movements to provide an evidence-based baseline for each next-quarter negotiation.

\subsection{Features designed for the customer segmentation}

The segmentation matrix contains one row per country--customer pair. In the current four-market sample this is nearly customer-level: the 82 rows correspond to 82 distinct SAP names, and only rare multinational groups appear as separate entities in more than one country (for example MicroPort in China and the US). Retaining country in the grain is nevertheless important. CeramTec can sell the same product to one customer group at different prices depending on the destination market, reflecting local reimbursement, procurement pressure, volumes, and commercial arrangements. This design therefore supports a future extension in which more multinational customers appear across several markets without incorrectly averaging their distinct pricing environments.

The feature families comprise SAP commercial and portfolio metrics (19), inferred contract-period dynamics (15), Salesforce engagement (12), external market context (28), country dummies (4), and structural flags (3), plus identifiers. Salesforce is matched for 66 of 82 accounts (80.5\%). The matrix covers China (43 rows), the US (17), France (14), and Italy (8), representing EUR\,690.5M in cumulative hip-bearing revenue and a median account-level unit price of EUR\,190.

\subsection{Features designed for predictive pricing}

The predictive-pricing panel contains one row per country--customer--product-group--quarter from 2020Q1 to 2026Q3: 1{,}744 observations across 27 quarters and 154 customer--product--country series. This is a workable sample for conventional tabular machine-learning models and supports a genuine temporal train/test split, although it remains too small to justify unnecessarily complex architectures. First observations are retained so thin-history customers remain priceable. Predictors include lagged and rolling prices, prior volume, market benchmarks at $t{-}1$, trend and momentum measures, and lagged external indicators; all time-dependent fields are computed from information available before the target quarter to prevent leakage. Where customer-specific history is unavailable, market benchmarks and median imputation keep the observation usable, as detailed in Phase~4.


\section{Phase 3: Market and Customer Segmentation}
\label{sec:phase3}

Phase~3 derives \textbf{pricing-behaviour segments}: groups of OEM accounts with similar commercial scale, contract dynamics, and engagement intensity. This is distinct from CeramTec's existing revenue/potential tiering, which remains useful for account-management resourcing.

\subsection{Methodology}

The segmenter uses 45 curated numeric features (16 SAP, 15 contract, 11 Salesforce, and 3 structural flags). Country dummies and country-constant external fields are excluded so geography does not dominate distance. Features are median-imputed and Robust-scaled. A singleton cluster (Double Medical) and a two-OEM micro-cluster (ITI Medical, b-ONE Medical) are removed because extreme low engagement distorts centroids, leaving $n{=}79$. K-Means with an elbow/silhouette sweep is the primary algorithm, with Ward hierarchical clustering as a robustness check and 2-D/3-D PCA for visualisation. After cleaning, $k{=}4$ is selected (silhouette $\approx 0.71$).

\subsection{Comparison with CeramTec's current segmentation}

CeramTec's current tiers combine revenue size and strategic potential. That logic is effective for allocating Key Account Managers, but it does not identify customers with similar pricing or negotiation behaviour. In the cleaned analysis, 11 of 12 PDF Key Accounts remain and are spread across three of the four behavioural clusters. Existing ``strategic importance'' therefore does \textbf{not} form a natural behavioural cluster: data-driven segments split accounts by engagement, growth, and contract dynamics rather than by the register label alone.

\subsection{Pricing implications}

Legacy Strategic Partners warrant value defence grounded in clinical and volume evidence, with joint planning as the default posture. Mid-Market Core accounts are suited to standard baselines indexed to market signals, with renewal discounts benchmarked against segment medians. Mature Diversified Accounts need explicit management of portfolio breadth and renewal complexity. High-Growth Challengers should pair volume gates with clearer expansion pricing rules.

Country remains a separate pricing axis: a US legacy partner and an Italian legacy partner share behavioural scale but face different payer ceilings.

\subsection{Constraints encountered}

Fuzzy SAP--Salesforce matching leaves some accounts CRM-thin; outlier removal is necessary but judgement-based; and cluster labels should be refreshed as the customer master improves.

\section{Phase 4: Pricing Predictive Model and Negotiation Support}
\label{sec:phase4}

Phase~4 builds a \textbf{next-quarter unit-price model} on the Phase~2 panel. The objective is directional negotiation support: forecast expected price, flag erosion, and quantify volume-for-discount trade-offs---not automated list-price setting.

\subsection{Modelling setup}

\textbf{Target.} Realised unit price (EUR) at country $\times$ customer $\times$ product group $\times$ quarter.\\
\textbf{Sample.} 1{,}744 panel rows; 35 predictors after excluding direct leakage of the contemporaneous target.\\
\textbf{Temporal split.} Train on 2020Q1--2025Q3 (1{,}516 rows); test on 2025Q4--2026Q3 (228 rows).\\
\textbf{Validation.} Five-fold \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit} during training (no random shuffle).\\
\textbf{Models compared.} Linear Regression, Random Forest, sklearn Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost.

\subsection{Pricing drivers and business value}

Permutation importance (Figure~\ref{fig:phase4_importance}) is dominated by previous-quarter price, four-quarter rolling mean price, cumulative average price, and lagged market mean/median prices. Secondary signals include volume, recent price change, and relationship maturity. Commercially, these drivers support next-quarter anchors, early erosion flags relative to the account's own history and market benchmark, and volume-for-discount trade-off analysis.

\subsection{Cold-start pricing for thin-history and new customers}

The framework must also guide \textbf{new or thin-history accounts}. The panel keeps every first observation of a country--customer--product series: when no prior quarter exists, own-price lags are missing and \texttt{n\_prior\_quarters}=0, but lagged country--product market benchmarks, product-group indicators, and customer-context fields remain. Missing lags are median-imputed so the Random Forest falls back to market and structural signals rather than dropping the row; after one prior quarter, own-price inertia enters the forecast.

Cold-start accuracy was validated explicitly. On the out-of-time test set, five rows with \texttt{n\_prior\_quarters}$\le$1 yield RMSE EUR\,11.4 and MAE EUR\,8.5, versus full-test RMSE EUR\,10.0 and MAE EUR\,5.4---a modest gap on a small hold-out. In-sample, 149 cold-start rows (RMSE EUR\,7.4) compare with 1{,}042 established accounts that have more than four prior quarters (RMSE EUR\,6.3), about $1.2\times$ the established error. True zero-history cases are harder (in-sample RMSE EUR\,23.6 on 12 rows, partly from atypical product lines), but error falls sharply once one prior quarter is observed (RMSE EUR\,3.4 on 137 rows). New-customer quotes should therefore be treated as market-anchored starting points with closer human review, while guidance tightens quickly as history accumulates.

\subsection{How to use the model in negotiation}

The model is a \textbf{negotiation support tool}, not a profit-optimal quote engine. The predicted next-quarter price should be read as an anchor relative to the account's recent realised price and the market benchmark. Accounts whose quotes deviate persistently beyond recent residual scale deserve commercial review. Phase~3 segment language should shape the negotiation tone---legacy partners and high-growth challengers need different playbooks---while country payer ceilings remain hard external constraints on what OEMs can pass through.

\subsection{Constraints encountered}

Production costs, FX hedging, duties, and formal contract clauses are absent, so predictions cannot be mapped directly to margin-optimal prices. Negotiation context in meeting notes is only partially structured. Geographic coverage is limited to four markets. High $R^2$ partly reflects strong price persistence; commercial review remains mandatory.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Three limitation classes bound interpretation. First, production costs, hedging, and taxes are absent, so outputs are price anchors rather than profit-optimal recommendations. Second, transactional SAP data and structured CRM fields omit full contractual terms; meeting notes would require deeper NLP to recover qualitative leverage. Third, results should not be generalised beyond US, China, France, and Italy without further validation, because healthcare systems differ enough that transfer without recalibration is unsafe.

Source comparability (registry versus VBP demand versus survey) and residual SAP--Salesforce matching gaps remain secondary but material data-quality constraints.

\section{Recommendations and Next Steps}
\label{sec:recommendations}

A data-driven pricing strategy is feasible for CeramTec if framed as evidence-based negotiation support and next-quarter price guidance. Structural market findings---post-COVID normalisation, near-inelastic component demand, contract stickiness, and OEM concentration---support customer- and product-level baselines rather than real-time dynamic pricing.

We recommend three implementation steps. First, validate segment labels, driver rankings, and contested accounts with Key Account Managers. Second, trial the model under shadow conditions by generating next-quarter anchors in parallel with live negotiations for one or two quarters without binding the sales force. Third, integrate production costs and contract details so anchors can evolve toward margin-aware guidance. In parallel, maintain the external register on a refresh schedule and establish a shared SAP--Salesforce customer master key.

\section{Project Learnings}
\label{sec:learnings}

\subsection{Healthcare market and regulation}

Medical-device pricing is mediated by institutions rather than spot markets. Cross-country comparison requires simultaneous attention to reimbursement (money-in) and procurement/tender (money-out) logics---illustrated by China's VBP regime, Italy's regional fragmentation, and France's dual GHS/LPPR structure.

\subsection{Project management and client delivery}

NDA delays and incremental data releases forced a parallel workstream: framework design and external collection first, internal modelling later. Reframing from dynamic pricing to negotiation baselines was itself a major consulting deliverable.

\subsection{Technical: agentic AI and platform agility}

Agentic AI accelerated scraping and PDF extraction, but only with disciplined human validation and explicit Partial/Todo flags. Combining Databricks for SAP joins with local reproducible notebooks reinforced platform agility across client and academic environments.

\section{Conclusion}

This project set out to assess the feasibility of a data-driven pricing strategy for CeramTec's ceramic hip bearings. We conclude that such a strategy is \textbf{feasible} when reframed as evidence-based negotiation baselines and next-quarter price guidance. All four phases are implemented: external evidence collection, feature engineering, pricing-behaviour segmentation, and a temporally validated Random Forest next-quarter model (out-of-time RMSE EUR\,10.0; MAE EUR\,5.4; $R^2{=}0.96$).

Literal dynamic pricing is not the right target. The durable assets are the maintainable evidence register, the two modelling datasets, the four behavioural segments, and the shadow-ready forecasting workflow---including cold-start support for thin-history accounts. Extending the model with costs and contract terms, then validating under shadow negotiation conditions, are the highest-value next steps.
