\section*{Executive Summary}

CeramTec can benefit from a data-driven pricing strategy, but not from real-time dynamic pricing. Its prices are negotiated with a concentrated group of original equipment manufacturer (OEM) customers through long-term commercial relationships, rather than adjusted continuously as in airlines or ticketing. We therefore recommend evidence-based negotiation baselines and next-quarter price guidance: use evidence on each customer's purchasing behaviour, relationship history, market environment, and product portfolio to prepare customer- and product-level anchors and identify the arguments that can best defend value.

A major source of business value is the creation of CeramTec's first structured external market-data capability. Before this engagement, the company had not systematically collected external evidence to complement its internal SAP and Salesforce records. We assembled fragmented public data across reimbursement and procurement, surgery volumes and waiting times, bearing preferences, clinical and revision outcomes, input-cost indicators, OEM performance, and competitive activity in the US, China, France, and Italy. This broader market view enables CeramTec to identify emerging trends, country-specific pricing pressure, and previously underexplored commercial opportunities that cannot be detected from internal transactions alone. The accompanying automated retrieval, cleaning, and aggregation pipelines turn this work into a repeatable capability rather than a one-off analysis.

The analysis confirms that customer size alone is not sufficient for pricing decisions. CeramTec's existing segmentation remains useful for allocating account-management resources, but customers with similar revenue can display materially different purchasing, engagement, and negotiation behaviours. The project therefore identifies four complementary pricing-behaviour segments and provides next-quarter price guidance at customer and product level. The principal commercial insight is that realised prices are strongly shaped by prior pricing, market position, purchasing volume, and relationship maturity. This supports a more consistent approach to renewal discussions, volume-for-discount trade-offs, price-erosion warnings, and the pricing of customers with limited history.

We recommend that CeramTec first validate the proposed segments and pricing drivers with Key Account Managers, then test the guidance for one or two negotiation cycles under shadow conditions, without making it binding on sales teams. Before operational deployment, production costs, contractual terms, foreign-exchange exposure, duties, and commercial judgement should be incorporated so that predicted market prices can be translated into margin-aware recommendations. The framework should remain a decision-support tool with human review, not an automated quoting engine.

Together, these deliverables form a reproducible four-phase framework covering market intelligence and data integration, feature engineering, customer segmentation, and predictive pricing support. The foundation can be refreshed and extended as new internal and external evidence becomes available.

\section{Introduction}

CeramTec is a global manufacturer of advanced technical ceramics, producing over 10,000 products across a range of industries, including electronics, energy and industrial manufacturing, and medical technology. Within its Medical Technology division, CeramTec supplies ceramic components for orthopaedic implants used in hip replacements.

This report assesses whether a data-driven pricing approach can improve CeramTec's commercial decisions for ceramic hip bearings across markets and customers. We evaluate the project's initial ``dynamic pricing'' framing, argue for evidence-based negotiation baselines and next-quarter price guidance as the appropriate objective (Section~\ref{sec:reframing}), and present a four-phase framework spanning evidence collection, feature engineering, customer segmentation, and predictive negotiation support.

CeramTec's ceramic bearings form part of the total hip implant system shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hip_implant}. Specifically, the bearing is the articulating interface between the femoral head and acetabular liner, where material choice influences wear, durability, and long-term implant performance.

\subsection{CeramTec in the value chain}

CeramTec's Medical Technology division recorded approximately EUR\,335M of the group's EUR\,685M revenue in 2025, with roughly 3{,}500 employees and 16 production sites. In hip arthroplasty (hip replacement surgery), CeramTec operates as a business-to-business (B2B) component supplier, manufacturing high-performance ceramic femoral heads and acetabular liners for original equipment manufacturers (OEMs). These OEMs assemble and commercialise the complete implant systems that are subsequently supplied to healthcare providers. Hospitals purchase these systems under country-specific reimbursement and procurement rules, meaning CeramTec's pricing is influenced by the downstream commercial constraints faced by its OEM customers.

\subsubsection{Customer base and competitive landscape}

CeramTec's customer base consists primarily of global and regional orthopaedic OEMs. Its internal Key Accounts register categorises these customers according to current and potential sales and strategic-project status. Large and medium accounts are managed by dedicated Key Account Managers, while smaller accounts are covered by field specialists or Customer Service. The Key Accounts register is examined further in Phase 3 (Section~\ref{sec:phase3}); Phases~2--4 use the broader SAP customer universe across the US, China (CN), France (FR), and Italy (IT) to avoid restricting the analysis to strategic accounts alone.

Phase~1 (Section~\ref{sec:external-data}) external profiling focused on five disclosure-rich OEMs (Stryker, DePuy Synthes, Zimmer Biomet, Smith\&Nephew, Medacta), to assess differences in scale, geographic exposure, product portfolios, and market positioning.

Within the competitive landscape, CeramTec is differentiated as a specialist ceramic-component supplier rather than a full-system implant manufacturer. Its BIOLOX\textregistered{} platform is estimated to account for over 90\% of the bearing market within this segment. Alternative suppliers include Kyocera Medical, CoorsTek, HiPer Medical, and Enovis/Mathys, alongside a growing number of China-based manufacturers. This strong market position limits direct competition but does not eliminate pressure from alternative bearing materials or OEM cost constraints.

\subsection{Hip bearing products}
CeramTec's hip bearing portfolio centres on BIOLOX\textregistered{} forte, an alumina ceramic, and BIOLOX\textregistered{} delta, a zirconia-toughened alumina composite. Advanced ceramics offer high hardness, low friction, wear resistance, and biocompatibility, supporting implant durability and long-term performance. BIOLOX\textregistered{} delta further improves fracture resistance relative to conventional alumina.

These components are supplied in ceramic-on-polyethylene (CoP) and ceramic-on-ceramic (CoC) bearing configurations. CoC offers very low wear, while CoP combines a ceramic femoral head with a polyethylene liner and represents the larger-volume ceramic segment in many markets.

Phase~1 registry and China's Volume-Based Procurement (VBP) proxy evidence indicates substantial variation in ceramic adoption across the US, China, France, and Italy. These differences influence CeramTec's market potential and provide an important market-level input to the pricing analysis.

\subsubsection{Current pricing approach}

CeramTec currently prices bearings primarily using a cost-plus approach, whereby production cost is combined with a target margin. Realised SAP prices already reflect some differentiation through volume discounts and account history; however, there is no systematic, evidence-based baseline incorporating market and customer characteristics. Given payer ceilings, procurement competition, and OEM heterogeneity, a single global cost-plus formula cannot capture willingness-to-pay differences across France's GHS/LPPR logic, Italy's regional fragmentation, US Medicare/commercial split, or China's Volume-Based Procurement (VBP) rounds.

\section{Project Scope and Methodology}

Against this background, CeramTec asked: Can existing data support future AI-enabled pricing decisions? The project therefore assessed the feasibility of a data-driven pricing approach by identifying key pricing drivers, evaluating available data and its limitations, and developing a roadmap for future implementation. Ultimately, the objective was to determine where analytics can add value to CeramTec's pricing process while reducing the risk of premature investment in an AI-based solution.

Several project delivery considerations influenced the sequencing and prioritisation of the work. Appendix~\ref{app:project-delivery-considerations} summarises these considerations and the measures taken to maintain progress.

\subsection{Problem reframing: negotiation baselines versus dynamic pricing}
\label{sec:reframing}

The project was initially framed around dynamic pricing. Early analysis of CeramTec's commercial model, however, indicated that real-time or high-frequency price adjustment was not well aligned with how ceramic bearings are sold.

Dynamic pricing is most applicable where prices can respond frequently to changes in demand, capacity, or timing, as in aviation or ticketing. CeramTec instead operates through a concentrated set of OEM customers, relationship-based contracts, and individually negotiated prices. Pricing decisions are therefore relatively infrequent and embedded within longer medical-device contracting and customer relationship cycles.

The project was therefore reframed towards evidence-based negotiation baselines and next-quarter price guidance, using customer, product, and market data to inform pricing discussions. This shifts analytics from automated price setting to decision support, while preserving commercial judgement, and forms the basis of the four-phase framework below.

\section{Phase 1: Data Collection and Exploration}
\label{sec:external-data}

\subsection{Approach}

No centralised external evidence base existed at the outset, only qualitative context from stakeholders. We therefore implemented reproducible acquisition and processing pipelines, designed to be re-executed as reimbursement schedules and procurement tenders evolve. Evidence spans thirteen thematic categories across four markets selected for representativeness: France and Italy (mature systems), the United States (fragmented, private-payer dominated) and China (largest, highest-growth). Each source is structured through a \emph{money-in} (reimbursement) versus \emph{money-out} (device tender) framework, isolating the layer at which a device price is actually observable.

\subsection{Reimbursement structure}

No market reimburses ceramic bearings distinctly from polyethylene: payment binds at the surgical episode or, in China, through procurement tenders rather than at the component level. Market structures differ substantially, however. China combines explicit implant ceilings with competitive bidding processes for companies on the \emph{money-out} side, following the structural break of the 2021 volume-based procurement reform. The United States has a diverse reimbursement mix involving many private payers, making an overall market view difficult to establish. France provides the cleanest and most transparent reimbursement structure, while Italy's highly regionalised system creates greater variation in payment mechanisms and ceilings. Consequently, any ceramic premium must be justified through clinical value rather than payer coding, with the relevant pricing mechanism depending strongly on market and channel.

\subsection{Bearing mix}

Bearing-surface adoption differs across markets as regulations and reimbursement policies shape clinical and commercial preferences in different ways. Ceramic involvement is elevated in Italy and China, intermediate in France, and lower in the United States, reflecting differences in both reimbursement structures and market practices. These regulatory and payment environments therefore influence the extent to which ceramic solutions are clinically embedded and commercially accessible. Addressable premium volume consequently concentrates in markets where ceramic adoption is already established.

Rising waiting times alongside flat-to-growing procedure volumes indicate that demand is constrained by surgical throughput rather than by price sensitivity.

\subsection{Internal Data: SalesForce behavioural signals}
 
With a behavioural layer — conversation around the contracts and transactions of roughly 1,500 records across six report exports, 223 accounts, and 380 meeting notes — the Salesforce meeting notes are the only internal source that records \emph{how} a price is reached rather than merely what it ended up being.
Read qualitatively, the notes are consistent: the same considerations
resurface across markets and accounts, and four dimensions shape
essentially every pricing conversation.
 
\emph{Component type} determines the starting point, as premium
components are discussed on different terms from base parts; the object
under negotiation, not the account alone, sets the reference. \emph{Order
size} operates as a central lever, with larger volumes associated with
greater unit-price concessions, so a discount is typically framed
against a volume commitment rather than granted outright. \emph{Customer
market} exerts a systematic pull, as Chinese customers anchor lower in
line with domestic competitive intensity and volume-based procurement
expectations rather than account-specific weakness. \emph{Competitor
pricing} sets a floor, as locally available alternatives create a
competitive reference point that customers raise directly in
discussion, particularly where domestic ceramic suppliers are active.
 
These signals are qualitative rather than numeric, and they are not a
substitute for the realised prices held in SAP. Their value is
interpretive: they explain why otherwise similar accounts may negotiate
differently, and they identify the arguments a negotiation-support tool
must be able to evidence. On this basis, component, order-size, market,
and competitive context were carried forward as engagement, market, and
product-mix features into Phase~2.

\subsection{Internal Data: SAP commercial patterns}
 
SAP constitutes the commercial layer, recording what customers pay and how much they buy: 55{,}240 invoiced transactions across 170 customers and 25 countries (about EUR\,2.17B), with hip-bearing analysis restricted to the Delta and Forte lines in the four focus markets.
 
\paragraph{Strong variations in customer pricing, driven by purchase volume.}
The decisive signal emerges from the ceramic ball, where unit prices vary strongly and systematically with purchase volume. Dispersion is pronounced: the highest-priced customer decile pays roughly twice the lowest, while that lowest decile absorbs some 52 times the volume at close to 50\% lower unit price. This gradient is driven by a single dominant lever, an upfront volume discount of order 30\%, rather than by post-invoice leakage.
 
\paragraph{Latent pricing power.}
Demand appears close to inelastic ($r = -0.14$): quantity responds only weakly to price, suggesting that observed discounts reflect established commercial practice more than market necessity. Combined with CeramTec's position as one of the few credible suppliers of ceramic bearings, this indicates genuine pricing power and a tangible margin opportunity. Realizing this opportunity, however, requires re-basing discounts on customer value rather than relying on purchase volume alone.

\subsection{Challenges and constraints encountered}

Regulated-market opacity constrained access across most of the external evidence base, from macro-financial, cost and reimbursement indicators to demand, clinical and competitive intelligence, leaving coverage uneven. We addressed this through reproducible pipelines and transparent, well-documented assumptions and proxies, converting a fragmented landscape into an auditable base. Collectively, these signals establish country and channel as structural pricing dimensions and reframe the objective from spot pricing toward negotiation-ready, segment-specific guidance. The exercise also yielded a durable insight: pricing signal resides at the episode and tender level, not the component. This backbone, on which Phases~2--4 depend, remains re-executable to support the later modelling phases.

The internal layers posed a different constraint. Salesforce evidence
sits in free-text notes rather than structured fields, so it reliably
signals which considerations drive a negotiation but does not support
robust numerical extraction---hence the behavioural findings are kept
qualitative. The same OEM also appears under several billing and CRM
entities, requiring manual normalisation and fuzzy matching that still
leaves some accounts CRM-thin. A shared customer master key is therefore
a prerequisite for turning these layers into a reproducible input to
Phases~2--4.

\section{Phase 2: Feature Engineering and Internal Data Integration}
\label{sec:phase2}

\subsection{Outlier identification}

All three accounts share thin Salesforce engagement: ITI Medical and b-ONE Medical each have only one logged meeting, while Double Medical has two meetings and no contact for more than five years. \subsection{Comparison with CeramTec's current classification}

If the Key Accounts register already captured pricing behaviour, strategic OEMs would concentrate in one or two clusters. They do not: named Key Accounts in the cleaned four-market matrix remain spread across three of the four behavioural segments, with only modest concentration in any single cluster. The practical implication is that strategic importance and negotiation behaviour are different axes. Keep the Key Accounts tiers for coverage and escalation; use the behavioural segments as an overlay when preparing negotiation baselines and playbooks.

\subsection{Commercial recommendations}

The four segments translate into distinct negotiation postures:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Legacy Strategic Partners}: defend value with clinical and volume evidence; default to joint planning; protect already-deep relationships.
    \item \textbf{Mid-Market Core}: apply standard baselines indexed to market signals; benchmark renewal discounts against segment medians rather than ad hoc exceptions.
    \item \textbf{Mature Diversified Accounts}: price portfolio breadth and multi-period renewals as a package, not line by line.
    \item \textbf{High-Growth Challengers}: pair clearer expansion pricing rules with volume or win-rate gates; investigate why meeting cadence remains thin despite strong opportunity outcomes.
\end{itemize}

Commercial teams should combine three signals in every negotiation: Key Account tier (who owns the relationship), behavioural segment (how to negotiate), and country market context (what the payer environment allows). A US legacy partner and an Italian legacy partner may share behavioural profile but still face different reimbursement ceilings.

If CeramTec prefers a lighter alternative to the Phase~4 predictive model, segment membership itself can support a simple reference-price system: for each product, use the average realised price within each behavioural segment as the negotiation baseline. Tracking those segment--product averages over time also provides an early warning of price erosion when a whole customer type drifts downward. Phase~4 remains the richer option when CeramTec wants next-quarter anchors at customer--product grain.

\subsection{Constraints encountered}

Fuzzy SAP--Salesforce matching leaves some accounts CRM-thin, the three outlier removals are necessary but partly judgement-based, and cluster labels should be refreshed as the customer master improves. The Key Accounts register is global and includes OEMs outside the four modelled markets, so not every named account maps one-to-one into the Phase~3 sample.

\subsection{How to use the model in negotiation}

The model is a negotiation support tool, not an automated quote engine. Commercial teams should use it to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Forecast next-quarter price} --- treat the prediction as an evidence-based anchor for the same country--customer--product combination.
    \item \textbf{Flag price erosion early} --- review accounts whose proposed price sits well below recent residual scale relative to their own history or the market benchmark.
    \item \textbf{Quantify volume-for-discount trade-offs} --- compare requested discounts with historical price--volume patterns for that account and product.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several constraints bound how the results should be interpreted.

\paragraph{No cost or contractual completeness.}
The analysis works on the revenue side alone, so outputs act as price anchors rather than margin-optimal recommendations: production costs, FX hedging, duties, and contract clauses stay outside the calculation. The internal data compounds this, capturing outcomes but not the negotiation context behind them. SAP and CRM fields record the transaction rather than its contractual terms, so deeper text analysis of meeting notes would be needed to recover qualitative leverage, and negotiation dynamics are therefore only partially reflected.
\vspace{0.4em}

\paragraph{Strong price persistence.}
The high $R^2$ partly reflects sticky realised prices, with the prior quarter's price the dominant driver. This mirrors a genuine feature of the market, since OEM contracts are long-term and relationship-based, but the model is best read as an extrapolation of recent commercial reality, not proof that price moves freely with every signal. Its outputs should frame a negotiation rather than settle it, so commercial review remains mandatory.
\vspace{0.4em}

\paragraph{Geographic scope.}
Results cover US, China, France, and Italy only. The pipeline is built to scale, but pricing drivers vary materially because reimbursement and procurement regimes differ across markets, so transfer elsewhere without recalibration is unsafe.
\vspace{0.4em}

\paragraph{Data quality.}
External sources differ in grain, mixing registry counts, VBP demand, and survey estimates, so cross-market comparisons carry added uncertainty. Residual SAP--Salesforce matching gaps leave some accounts CRM-thin, and Phase~3 outlier removals remain partly judgement-based. Cold-start product lines are the hardest case, so thin-history quotes need closer human oversight.

\section{Recommendations and Next Steps}
 
A data-driven pricing strategy is feasible for CeramTec if framed as
evidence-based negotiation support and next-quarter price guidance.
Four structural market findings support this conclusion: the COVID-era
demand shock has been absorbed, component-level demand is
near-inelastic, contracts are sticky, and the customer base is
concentrated in a small number of major OEMs. Together these rule out
real-time dynamic pricing and favour customer- and product-level
baselines prepared before each negotiation. The model should therefore
be read as negotiation support: not a real-time pricing engine, but a
tool that identifies the key levers---price history, volume,
relationship maturity, and market context---for each customer. The
steps below are sequenced so that each stage de-risks the next.
 
\textbf{Validate findings with commercial stakeholders.} Before any
operational use, review the four negotiation dimensions
(Section~4.2), the segment labels and driver rankings (Section~6), and
contested accounts with Key Account Managers. Two checks matter most:
whether the volume-for-discount patterns observed in SAP and quoted in
meeting notes reflect actual contracting structures, and whether the
competitor price floors reported by Chinese customers remain current.
The three dormant outlier accounts identified in Section~6.3 should be
investigated in the same pass. This step costs little and prevents the
framework from encoding stale or misread commercial reality.
 
\textbf{Integrate production costs and contract details.} The current
model predicts realised market prices; it does not know CeramTec's
margins. Incorporating production costs, contractual terms, duties,
and foreign-exchange exposure converts price anchors into
margin-aware recommendations, and is the precondition for using the
guidance in price-setting rather than price-benchmarking. A shared
SAP--Salesforce customer master key is the enabling data step: fuzzy
matching currently leaves roughly a fifth of segmentation accounts
CRM-thin, and every downstream output inherits that gap.
 
\textbf{Trial the model under shadow conditions.} Run the Phase~4
guidance in parallel with live negotiations for one or two cycles
without binding the sales force. For each negotiation, generate the
baseline before the meeting and have the Key Account Manager record
acceptance, override, and rationale. This serves two purposes at once:
it tests trust and accuracy under real conditions, and it produces the
structured negotiation-outcome data that the current CRM does not
capture---closing the largest behavioural-data gap identified in
Phase~1.
 
Throughout, the framework should remain decision support with human
review: analytical recommendation, plus Key Account Manager context,
plus contract and cost constraints, produce the negotiation
decision---never an automated quoting engine.

\section{Conclusion and Key Learnings}

This project set out to test whether a data-driven pricing strategy is feasible for CeramTec's ceramic hip bearings. It is \textbf{feasible}, once reframed from literal dynamic pricing to evidence-based negotiation baselines and next-quarter guidance. All four phases are implemented: external evidence collection, feature engineering, pricing-behaviour segmentation, and a temporally validated Random Forest next-quarter model (out-of-time RMSE EUR\,10.0, MAE EUR\,5.4, $R^2{=}0.96$). The durable assets are the maintainable evidence register, the two modelling datasets, the four behavioural segments, and the shadow-ready forecasting workflow, including cold-start support for thin-history accounts. The highest-value next steps are to extend the model with costs and contract terms, then validate it under shadow negotiation conditions. 

The project delivered technical output, but its main value for the team lay in three transferable areas:

\paragraph{Healthcare market and regulation.}
We learned to navigate a highly regulated and fragmented healthcare market in which medical-device pricing is mediated by institutions rather than spot markets. Building usable context from scratch required deep-dive research across reimbursement, procurement, and clinical evidence, and it forced us to adapt to an industry whose economics shift markedly from one country to the next, so that a conclusion drawn in France rarely transferred directly to China. Cross-country comparison in particular demanded simultaneous attention to reimbursement (money-in) and procurement or tender (money-out) logics, illustrated by China's VBP regime, Italy's regional fragmentation, and France's dual GHS and LPPR structure.
\vspace{0.4em}

\paragraph{Project management and client delivery.}
The engagement behaved like a real consulting mandate rather than an academic exercise. NDA delays and incremental data releases forced a parallel workstream, with framework design and external collection running first and internal modelling following once access opened. We coordinated within the team and directly with the client through those shifting constraints, and much of the work consisted of bridging technical and commercial stakeholders, translating analytical findings into guidance a negotiation team can act on. Reframing the brief from dynamic pricing to negotiation baselines was itself a major consulting deliverable, not a detour.
\vspace{0.4em}

\paragraph{Technical: agentic AI and platform agility.}
We strengthened our applied analytics practice by working within the client's own Databricks environment and automating collection and processing through reproducible scripts, so that the pipelines can be re-executed and extended as markets, tariffs, and tenders evolve. Agentic AI accelerated scraping and PDF extraction, but only with disciplined human validation and explicit Partial and Todo flags to keep unverified outputs visible. Combining Databricks for the SAP joins with local reproducible notebooks reinforced platform agility across both client and academic environments.

\section{Project Delivery Considerations}
\label{app:project-delivery-considerations}
The project was delivered within several practical constraints that influenced the sequencing and prioritisation of the work. The engagement began on 17 June with an initial focus on assessing the feasibility of dynamic pricing. Access to CeramTec's internal systems required completion of NDA and onboarding processes, with access to Databricks, the company's internal data platform, established on 8 July. To maintain progress during this period, external data collection, market research, and development of the analytical framework were progressed in parallel. Internal data became available from 9 July and was subsequently integrated with the external evidence base as access expanded. This required an iterative delivery approach, with the analytical framework developed first and progressively enriched through internal commercial and behavioural data. In parallel, continued engagement with CeramTec stakeholders led to an evolution of the original project scope. Early analysis indicated that frequent dynamic price adjustment was not well aligned with CeramTec's relationship-based OEM contracting model. The project was therefore reframed towards evidence-based negotiation baselines and next-quarter price guidance, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:reframing}. This extended the work beyond the initial feasibility assessment towards the development and implementation of a broader pricing decision framework. Finally, the project was delivered by a team of four students rather than the anticipated six to eight. Given the evolving scope and phased data availability, this increased the importance of prioritising the highest-value workstreams and working iteratively. Collectively, these considerations shaped the delivery approach without changing the project's overarching objective, resulting in the phased framework presented in the main report.

\subsection{Feature separation ($\eta^2$)}

To rank which features most separate the four segments, we use $\eta^2$: the share of \emph{that feature's} variance that lies between segments rather than within them,
\begin{equation}
\eta^{2}
=
\frac{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{between}}}{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{total}}}
=
\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} n_{k}(\bar{x}_{k}-\bar{x})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{2}},
\end{equation}
where $x_{i}$ is the feature value for account $i$, $\bar{x}$ is the overall mean, $\bar{x}_{k}$ and $n_{k}$ are the mean and size of segment $k$, and $K{=}4$. These scores are feature-specific and do \textbf{not} sum to 100\%---unlike normalised model importances in Phase~4. Figure~\ref{fig:phase3_importance} shows the ranking: engagement recency dominates, followed by portfolio expansion at renewal and commercial scale.
