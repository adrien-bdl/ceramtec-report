\section*{Executive Summary}

CeramTec can benefit from a data-driven pricing strategy, but not from real-time dynamic pricing. Its prices are negotiated with a concentrated group of original equipment manufacturer (OEM) customers through long-term commercial relationships, rather than adjusted continuously as in airlines or ticketing. We therefore recommend evidence-based negotiation baselines and next-quarter price guidance: use evidence on each customer's purchasing behaviour, relationship history, market environment, and product portfolio to prepare customer- and product-level anchors and identify the arguments that can best defend value.

A major source of business value is the creation of CeramTec's first structured external market-data capability. Before this engagement, the company had not systematically collected external evidence to complement its internal SAP and Salesforce records. We assembled fragmented public data across reimbursement and procurement, surgery volumes and waiting times, bearing preferences, clinical and revision outcomes, input-cost indicators, OEM performance, and competitive activity in the US, China, France, and Italy. This broader market view enables CeramTec to identify emerging trends, country-specific pricing pressure, and previously underexplored commercial opportunities that cannot be detected from internal transactions alone. The accompanying automated retrieval, cleaning, and aggregation pipelines turn this work into a repeatable capability rather than a one-off analysis.

The analysis confirms that customer size alone is not sufficient for pricing decisions. CeramTec's existing segmentation remains useful for allocating account-management resources, but customers with similar revenue can display materially different purchasing, engagement, and negotiation behaviours. The project therefore identifies four complementary pricing-behaviour segments and provides next-quarter price guidance at customer and product level. The principal commercial insight is that realised prices are strongly shaped by prior pricing, market position, purchasing volume, and relationship maturity. This supports a more consistent approach to renewal discussions, volume-for-discount trade-offs, price-erosion warnings, and the pricing of customers with limited history.

We recommend that CeramTec first validate the proposed segments and pricing drivers with Key Account Managers, then test the guidance for one or two negotiation cycles under shadow conditions, without making it binding on sales teams. Before operational deployment, production costs, contractual terms, foreign-exchange exposure, duties, and commercial judgement should be incorporated so that predicted market prices can be translated into margin-aware recommendations. The framework should remain a decision-support tool with human review, not an automated quoting engine.

Together, these deliverables form a reproducible four-phase framework covering market intelligence and data integration, feature engineering, customer segmentation, and predictive pricing support. The foundation can be refreshed and extended as new internal and external evidence becomes available.

\section{Introduction}

CeramTec is a global manufacturer of advanced technical ceramics, producing over 10,000 products across industries including electronics, energy, industrial manufacturing, and medical technology. Within its Medical Technology division, CeramTec supplies ceramic components for orthopaedic hip implants.

This report assesses whether a data-driven pricing approach can improve CeramTec's commercial decisions for ceramic hip bearings across markets and customers. We evaluate the project's initial ``dynamic pricing'' framing, argue for evidence-based negotiation baselines and next-quarter guidance (Section~\ref{sec:reframing}), and present a four-phase framework spanning evidence collection, feature engineering, customer segmentation, and predictive negotiation support.

CeramTec's ceramic bearings form part of the total hip implant system shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hip_implant}. The bearing is the articulating interface between the femoral head and acetabular liner, where material choice influences wear, durability, and long-term implant performance.

\subsubsection{Customer base and competitive landscape}

CeramTec's customer base consists primarily of global and regional orthopaedic OEMs. Its internal Key Accounts register classifies customers according to current and potential sales and strategic relevance, while Phase~3 (Section~\ref{sec:phase3}) complements this view with behavioural segmentation. Phases~2--4 use the broader SAP customer universe across the US, China, France, and Italy.

Within the competitive landscape, CeramTec is differentiated as a specialist ceramic-component supplier, with its BIOLOX\textregistered{} platform estimated to account for over 90\% of the relevant bearing segment. This strong position limits direct competition, although pressure remains from alternative bearing materials, regional suppliers, and growing China-based manufacturers.


\subsection{Hip bearing products}

CeramTec's hip bearing portfolio centres on BIOLOX\textregistered{} forte, an alumina ceramic, and BIOLOX\textregistered{} delta, a zirconia-toughened alumina composite. Both offer low friction, wear resistance, and biocompatibility, while BIOLOX\textregistered{} delta provides greater fracture resistance.

These components are supplied in ceramic-on-polyethylene (CoP) and ceramic-on-ceramic (CoC) configurations. CoP represents the larger-volume ceramic segment in many markets, while CoC remains relevant where very low wear is prioritised.

Phase~1 evidence indicates substantial variation in ceramic adoption across the US, China, France, and Italy. These differences influence CeramTec's market potential and provide an important market-level input to the pricing analysis.

\subsubsection{Current pricing approach}

CeramTec currently prices bearings primarily using a cost-plus approach, combining production cost with a target margin. Realised SAP prices already reflect some differentiation through volume discounts and account history; however, there is no systematic, evidence-based baseline incorporating market and customer characteristics. Given differences in reimbursement structures, procurement regimes, and OEM profiles across markets, a single global cost-plus formula cannot fully capture variation in willingness-to-pay.

\section{Project Scope and Methodology}

CeramTec asked whether existing data could support future AI-enabled pricing decisions. We assessed feasibility by identifying key pricing drivers, evaluating data limitations, and building a maintainable framework for negotiation baselines without premature investment in automated quoting. Delivery constraints are summarised in Appendix~\ref{app:project-delivery-considerations}.

\subsection{Problem reframing: negotiation baselines versus dynamic pricing}
\label{sec:reframing}

The project was initially framed around dynamic pricing. Early analysis showed that high-frequency adjustment is poorly aligned with CeramTec's concentrated OEM base, relationship-based contracts, and individually negotiated prices embedded in longer medical-device cycles.

The objective was therefore reframed toward evidence-based negotiation baselines and next-quarter price guidance, using customer, product, and market data to support commercial judgement rather than automated price setting.

\section{Phase 1: Data Collection and Exploration}
\label{sec:external-data}

\subsection{Approach}

No centralised external evidence base existed at the outset, only qualitative context from stakeholders. We therefore implemented reproducible acquisition and processing pipelines, designed to be re-executed as reimbursement schedules and procurement tenders evolve. Evidence spans thirteen thematic categories across four markets selected for representativeness: France and Italy (mature systems), the United States (fragmented, private-payer dominated) and China (largest, highest-growth). Each source is structured through a \emph{money-in} (reimbursement) versus \emph{money-out} (device tender) framework, isolating the layer at which a device price is actually observable.

\subsection{Reimbursement structure}

Payment reaches the implant through two channels: \emph{money-in}, what a payer reimburses for the surgical episode, and \emph{money-out}, the price a hospital pays its supplier under competitive tenders. However, market structures differ substantially: China combines explicit implant ceilings with competitive bidding processes for companies on the \emph{money-out} side, following the structural break of the 2021 volume-based procurement reform. The United States has a diverse reimbursement mix involving many private payers, making an overall market view difficult to establish. France provides the cleanest and most transparent reimbursement structure, while Italy's highly regionalised system creates greater variation in payment mechanisms and ceilings. Consequently, any ceramic premium must be first justified through clinical value, then priced through each market's specific mechanism.

\subsection{Bearing mix}

Bearing-surface adoption differs across markets as regulations and reimbursement policies shape clinical and commercial preferences in different ways. Ceramic involvement is elevated in Italy and China, intermediate in France, and lower in the United States, reflecting differences in both reimbursement structures and market practices. These regulatory and payment environments therefore influence the extent to which ceramic solutions are clinically embedded and commercially accessible. Addressable premium volume consequently concentrates in markets where ceramic adoption is already established.

\subsection{Demand and price formation}

Rising waiting times alongside flat-to-growing procedure volumes indicate that demand is constrained by surgical throughput rather than by price sensitivity.
This favours pricing anchored in volume commitments and contractual continuity over frequent repricing. \subsection{Internal Data: SAP commercial patterns}
 
SAP constitutes the commercial layer, recording what customers pay and how much they buy: 55{,}240 invoiced transactions across 170 customers and 25 countries (about EUR\,2.17B), with hip-bearing analysis restricted to the Delta and Forte lines in the four focus markets.
 
\paragraph{Strong variations in customer pricing, driven by purchase volume.}
The decisive signal emerges from the ceramic ball, where unit prices vary strongly and systematically with purchase volume. Dispersion is pronounced: the highest-priced customer decile pays roughly twice the lowest, while that lowest decile absorbs some 52 times the volume at close to 50\% lower unit price. This gradient is driven by a single dominant lever, an upfront volume discount of order 30\%, rather than by post-invoice leakage.
 
\paragraph{Latent pricing power.}
Demand appears close to inelastic ($r = -0.14$): quantity responds only weakly to price, suggesting that observed discounts reflect established commercial practice more than market necessity. Combined with CeramTec's position as one of the few credible suppliers of ceramic bearings, this indicates genuine pricing power and a tangible margin opportunity. Realizing this opportunity, however, requires re-basing discounts on customer value rather than relying on purchase volume alone.

\subsection{Challenges and constraints encountered}

Regulated-market opacity constrained access across the external evidence base, from cost and reimbursement indicators to demand, clinical, and competitive intelligence, leaving coverage uneven. We addressed this through reproducible pipelines and transparent, documented assumptions and proxies, converting a fragmented landscape into an auditable base. These signals establish country and channel as structural pricing dimensions and reinforce a key insight: observable pricing signals reside at the episode and tender level rather than the component level. This evidence base remains re-executable and supports Phases~2--4.

The internal layers posed a different constraint. Salesforce evidence sits in free-text notes rather than structured fields, identifying which considerations drive a negotiation but not supporting robust numerical extraction; behavioural findings are therefore kept qualitative. The same OEM may also appear under several billing and CRM entities, requiring normalisation and fuzzy matching and leaving some accounts CRM-thin. A shared customer master key is therefore important for reproducible integration across Phases~2--4.

\subsection{Commercial recommendations}

Named Key Accounts remain spread across three of four behavioural segments, so strategic importance and negotiation behaviour are different axes: keep Key Accounts for coverage; use behavioural segments as the negotiation overlay.

\section{Phase 4: Pricing Predictive Model and Negotiation Support}
\label{sec:phase4}

Phase~4 builds a next-quarter unit-price model on the Phase~2 panel. The objective is a negotiation baseline for each country $\times$ customer $\times$ product group, plus identification of the drivers sales teams should emphasise in discussion.

\subsection{Modelling setup}

\textbf{Target.} Realised unit price (EUR) at country $\times$ customer $\times$ product group $\times$ quarter.\\
\textbf{Sample.} 1{,}744 panel rows; 35 predictors after excluding direct leakage of the contemporaneous target.\\
\textbf{Temporal split.} Train on 2020Q1--2025Q3 (1{,}516 rows); out-of-time test on 2025Q4--2026Q3 (228 rows).\\
\textbf{Validation.} Five-fold \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit} during training (no random shuffle).\\
\textbf{Models compared.} Linear Regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost.

\subsection{Model comparison}

\subsection{How to use the model in negotiation}

The model is a negotiation support tool, not an automated quote engine. Commercial teams should use it to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Forecast next-quarter price} --- treat the prediction as an evidence-based anchor for the same country--customer--product combination.
    \item \textbf{Flag price erosion early} --- review accounts whose proposed price sits well below recent residual scale relative to their own history or the market benchmark.
    \item \textbf{Quantify volume-for-discount trade-offs} --- compare requested discounts with historical price--volume patterns for that account and product.
    \item \textbf{Price new or thin-history customers} --- start from market and product signals, with tighter human review until one or two realised quarters exist.
\end{itemize}

Phase~3 segment language should shape negotiation tone (e.g.\ legacy partners versus high-growth challengers), while country payer ceilings remain hard external constraints. Broader data and generalisation limits are summarised in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several constraints bound how the results should be interpreted.

\paragraph{No cost or contractual completeness.}
The analysis works on the revenue side alone, so outputs act as price anchors rather than margin-optimal recommendations: production costs, FX hedging, duties, and contract clauses stay outside the calculation. Internal data captures outcomes but not the negotiation context. SAP and CRM record transactions rather than contractual terms, so deeper analysis of meeting notes would be needed to recover qualitative leverage, leaving negotiation dynamics only partially reflected.
\vspace{0.4em}

\paragraph{Strong price persistence.}
The high $R^2$ partly reflects sticky realised prices, with the prior quarter's price the dominant driver. This reflects long-term, relationship-based OEM contracts, but the model is best read as an extrapolation of recent commercial reality, not proof that price moves freely with every signal. Outputs should frame rather than settle negotiations, so commercial review remains mandatory.
\vspace{0.4em}

\paragraph{Geographic scope.}
Results cover US, China, France, and Italy only. The pipeline is built to scale, but pricing drivers vary materially because reimbursement and procurement regimes differ across markets, so transfer elsewhere without recalibration is unsafe.
\vspace{0.4em}

\paragraph{Data quality.}
External sources differ in grain, mixing registry counts, VBP demand, and survey estimates, so cross-market comparisons carry added uncertainty. Residual SAP--Salesforce matching gaps leave some accounts CRM-thin, and Phase~3 outlier removals remain partly judgement-based. Cold-start product lines are the hardest case, so thin-history quotes need closer human oversight.



\clearpage

\section{Recommendations and Next Steps}

A data-driven pricing strategy is feasible for CeramTec if framed as evidence-based negotiation support and next-quarter price guidance. Near-inelastic component demand, sticky contracts, and a concentrated OEM base rule out real-time dynamic pricing and favour customer- and product-level baselines prepared before each negotiation. The steps below are sequenced so each stage de-risks the next.

\vspace{0.4em}

\textbf{Validate findings with commercial stakeholders.} Review segment labels, driver rankings, and contested accounts with Key Account Managers before operational use. Confirm that volume-for-discount patterns and competitor floors still match current contracting practice, and clarify the three dormant outlier accounts from Phase~3. This step costs little and prevents the framework from encoding stale commercial reality.

\vspace{0.4em}

\textbf{Integrate production costs and contract details.} The model predicts realised market prices, not CeramTec margins. Adding production costs, contractual terms, duties, and FX exposure turns anchors into margin-aware guidance. In parallel, establish a shared SAP--Salesforce customer master key: fuzzy matching still leaves some accounts CRM-thin, and every downstream output inherits that gap.

\vspace{0.4em}

\textbf{Trial the model under shadow conditions.} Run Phase~4 guidance in parallel with live negotiations for one or two cycles without binding the sales force. Generate the baseline before each meeting and record acceptance, override, and rationale. This tests trust under real conditions and captures the structured negotiation outcomes missing from today's CRM.

\vspace{0.4em}

Throughout, keep the framework as decision support with human review: analytical recommendation, Key Account Manager context, and cost or contract constraints jointly produce the negotiation decision---never an automated quoting engine.

\section{Conclusion and Key Learnings}


This project shows that a data-driven pricing strategy for CeramTec's ceramic hip bearings is \textbf{feasible} once reframed from literal dynamic pricing to evidence-based negotiation baselines and next-quarter guidance. All four phases are implemented: external evidence collection, feature engineering, pricing-behaviour segmentation, and a temporally validated Random Forest next-quarter model (out-of-time RMSE EUR\,10.0, MAE EUR\,5.4, $R^2{=}0.96$). The durable assets are the evidence register, modelling datasets, behavioural segments, and shadow-ready forecasting workflow, including cold-start support. Adding costs and contract terms, then validating through shadow negotiations, are the highest-value next steps.

Beyond the technical deliverables, the engagement developed three transferable capabilities:

\vspace{0.4em}

\paragraph{Healthcare market and regulation.}
Medical-device pricing is mediated by institutions rather than spot markets. Building usable context required research across reimbursement, procurement, and clinical evidence and adaptation to country-specific economics. Cross-country comparison demanded simultaneous attention to reimbursement (money-in) and procurement or tender (money-out) logics, illustrated by China's VBP regime, Italy's regional fragmentation, and France's dual GHS and LPPR structure.

\vspace{0.4em}

\paragraph{Project management and client delivery.}
NDA delays and incremental data releases required parallel workstreams---framework design and external collection first, internal modelling once access opened---and coordination between technical and commercial stakeholders. Reframing the brief from dynamic pricing to negotiation baselines was itself a major consulting deliverable, not a detour.

\vspace{0.4em}

\paragraph{Technical: agentic AI and platform agility.}
Databricks and reproducible scripts made the pipelines re-executable as markets and tenders evolve. Agentic AI accelerated scraping and PDF extraction, but only with disciplined human validation and explicit Partial and Todo flags. Combining Databricks for SAP joins with local notebooks reinforced platform agility across client and academic settings.

\section{Project Delivery Considerations}
\label{app:project-delivery-considerations}
The project was delivered within several practical constraints that influenced the sequencing and prioritisation of the work. The engagement began on 17 June with an initial focus on assessing the feasibility of dynamic pricing. Access to CeramTec's internal systems required completion of NDA and onboarding processes, with access to Databricks, the company's internal data platform, established on 8 July. To maintain progress during this period, external data collection, market research, and development of the analytical framework were progressed in parallel. Internal data became available from 9 July and was subsequently integrated with the external evidence base as access expanded. This required an iterative delivery approach, with the analytical framework developed first and progressively enriched through internal commercial and behavioural data. In parallel, continued engagement with CeramTec stakeholders led to an evolution of the original project scope. Early analysis indicated that frequent dynamic price adjustment was not well aligned with CeramTec's relationship-based OEM contracting model. The project was therefore reframed towards evidence-based negotiation baselines and next-quarter price guidance, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:reframing}. This extended the work beyond the initial feasibility assessment towards the development and implementation of a broader pricing decision framework. Finally, the project was delivered by a team of four students rather than the anticipated six to eight. Given the evolving scope and phased data availability, this increased the importance of prioritising the highest-value workstreams and working iteratively. Collectively, these considerations shaped the delivery approach without changing the project's overarching objective, resulting in the phased framework presented in the main report.

\label{app:phase3-diagnostics}

\subsection{Choice of $k$}

On the cleaned feature space ($n{=}79$), both the inertia elbow and the silhouette score peak at four clusters (Figure~\ref{fig:phase3_elbow}).

\centering

\subsection{Feature separation ($\eta^2$)}

To rank which features most separate the four segments, we use $\eta^2$: the share of \emph{that feature's} variance that lies between segments rather than within them,
\begin{equation}
\eta^{2}
=
\frac{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{between}}}{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{total}}}
=
\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} n_{k}(\bar{x}_{k}-\bar{x})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{2}},
\end{equation}
where $x_{i}$ is the feature value for account $i$, $\bar{x}$ is the overall mean, $\bar{x}_{k}$ and $n_{k}$ are the mean and size of segment $k$, and $K{=}4$. These scores are feature-specific and do \textbf{not} sum to 100\%---unlike normalised model importances in Phase~4. Figure~\ref{fig:phase3_importance} shows the ranking: engagement recency dominates, followed by portfolio expansion at renewal and commercial scale.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{6_phase3/phase3_feature_importance.png}
\caption{Per-feature $\eta^2$ for the cleaned four-segment solution: each bar is the between-cluster share of that feature's own variance (values are independent and do not sum to 100\%).}

\end{figure}
